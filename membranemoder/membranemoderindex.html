Model

The film is treated as an ideal membrane: no bending stiffness, uniform isotropic tension T, surface mass σ = ρh, edges clamped. Its modes are φ = sin(mπx/Lx)·sin(nπy/Ly) with wavenumber k = π√((m/Lx)² + (n/Ly)²). In vacuum f = k·√(T/σ) / 2π.

Drive area. The 1 N force is spread evenly over the drive area, so each mode is excited by the average of its shape over that area. For a round drive of radius a, that average is φ(x₀,y₀)·2J₁(ka)/(ka). For a rectangle of size w × h, it's φ(x₀,y₀)·sinc(mπw/2Lx)·sinc(nπh/2Ly). Modes whose wavelength is shorter than the drive average out, so a larger drive acts as a spatial low-pass filter. A centred drive still only excites odd–odd modes.

Air loading. Each mode gets the radiation impedance of a travelling wave with the same wavenumber on an infinite film, on both sides of the film. If the mode's wave is slower than sound (k > k₀), the air acts as an added mass 2ρ₀/√(k² − k₀²). If it's faster (k < k₀), the air acts as a radiation resistance 2ρ₀c₀/√(1 − k²/k₀²). This ignores coupling between modes through the air, stators, perforated plates and enclosures.

Damping patches. Inside a patch, the model adds up to four things: surface mass μ, bending stiffness D(1+iηD) (using ∇²φ = −k²φ, so the term is D·kᵢ²kⱼ²∫φᵢφⱼ), a loss factor ηp on the film's local tension energy (a viscoelastic layer that strains with the film), and an optional viscous resistance R (like felt touching a stator). Because these act only inside the patch, they couple modes together. The coupling integrals ∫φᵢφⱼ and ∫∇φᵢ·∇φⱼ are exact over each patch (closed form for rectangles, Bessel functions for circles). The membrane view solves the 400 bare-film modes nearest the selected frequency as one fully coupled system; the frequency response uses the nearest 60. All other modes keep only their own patch terms, without coupling to each other. Below a few hundred Hz this is essentially exact. At high frequency it underestimates how sharply the patches localise the motion. Patches are capped at 40% of the film area.

Radiation pattern. In an infinite baffle the far-field pressure is p(θ,ϕ) = ω²ρ₀·W̃(k sinθ cosϕ, k sinθ sinϕ)/(2πr), where W̃ is the 2-D spatial Fourier transform of the film displacement. Each mode's transform has a closed form, so the pattern in each principal plane is exact for the solved modal amplitudes. For the unbaffled film (a dipole), the baffled pattern is multiplied by the obliquity factor |cos θ| (Kirchhoff thin-screen approximation), and the rear lobe mirrors the front with inverted polarity. This approximation gives the pattern, not the level: it ignores diffraction around the edges, the front–back cancellation that lowers bass output, and any stators. Each octave is the power average of 24 frequencies across the band (1/24-octave spacing). Levels are sampled every 2° around the full circle and given in dB relative to the on-axis (0°) level of the same octave. In the dipole case, ±90° is a null and the rear (90°–270°) mirrors the front. In the baffle case nothing radiates behind the baffle, so those angles are blank.

Seen as a distributed-mode loudspeaker. The film works as a DML. Above the frequency where neighbouring driven modes overlap (modal overlap nd(f)·η·f ≥ 1, with nd the density of driven modes), the response and the radiation come from many modes at once rather than one at a time. That is why the frequency response smooths out and the patterns stop changing shape from one frequency to the next. Unlike a bending-wave DML plate, a membrane has no dispersion: its waves travel at √(T/σ) at every frequency. For this film that's 120 m/s, below the speed of sound, so the film never reaches coincidence and every resonant mode on its own is a poor radiator. The sound that does get out comes from edge and corner regions, from the low-wavenumber part of the drive area, and from cancellation that is incomplete across the band. So at high frequencies the drive size and position set the beam as much as the film size does. Damping patches broaden each mode, which raises the modal overlap and evens out the patterns.

Edge damping. The film is clamped, so its motion goes to zero at the frame: in a band of width b, a mode's motion only reaches about sin(kb) of its peak. A damping band is therefore almost invisible to long wavelengths (kb ≪ 1) and becomes effective once b is around a quarter wavelength or more. With a 3 cm band on this film that starts around 1–2 kHz. In that range the band absorbs waves that would otherwise reflect off the frame and build up resonances. The film then behaves more like a travelling-wave radiator: resonance peaks flatten and the response gets smoother. Below that, the edge band hardly changes the bass modes. The Tapered profile raises the damping in 5 steps toward the edge, which reflects less from the band's inner boundary than an abrupt uniform band. The band is modelled as frames (an outer rectangle minus an inner one) with the same exact integrals as the patches, and it counts toward the 40% coverage limit.

100 µm Mylar patch. The preset assumes 100 µm PET (139 g/m²) bonded with about 25 µm of acrylic pressure-sensitive adhesive (PSA, about 25 g/m²), 164 g/m² in total. That is about 24 times the mass of the 5 µm film. Its bending stiffness is D = Eh³/12(1−ν²) ≈ 440 µN·m (E ≈ 4.5 GPa, ν ≈ 0.38). On the 100 N/m film, bending matches tension at k = √(T/D) ≈ 480 rad/m (13 mm wavelength, about 8–9 kHz). Above that the patch acts as a stiff, heavy plate and blocks the film's waves; below it, the patch acts mainly as added mass. The loss factors are modest estimates for a thin PSA bond. The bonded layer is assumed to carry no pre-tension, and the patch edges are not modelled as free plate edges.

Dominant modes. "Share of motion" is each mode's |qᵢ|² as a fraction of the total (the spatial mean-square displacement). "Share of SPL" is |qᵢIᵢ|/|ΣqI|, where Iᵢ is the mode's net volume displacement. Shares above 100% mean that mode is being partly cancelled by others of opposite phase.
